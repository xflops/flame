# Search-R1 on Flame: a complete 100-step run with two trainers

## Assessment

A full 100-step Search-R1-style GRPO run (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, HotpotQA questions, BM25 retrieval) completed on a Flame Kubernetes cluster with 16 GPUs on two nodes: 14 rollout executors and 2 data-parallel trainer executors, all as `flamepy.app` services. No rollout call was dropped. The two trainers are a gang, started together by gang scheduling for `min_instances` (#593). Held-out exact match on 200 dev questions rose from **0.145** (untrained) to **0.47** (step 100), and the mean training reward rose from 0.33 over the first 10 steps to 0.58 over the last 10.

This is one run with one seed. The held-out set is 200 questions, so each exact-match value carries a standard error of about 0.035; the gain from 0.145 to 0.47 is far larger than that, but the differences between neighbouring evaluations (0.41, 0.45, 0.47) are not.

## Setup

- **Model and algorithm:** Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, synchronous on-policy GRPO, bf16 weight publication as deltas, fp32 master weights on the trainers, vLLM 0.31.0 for rollouts. Learning rate 1e-6, KL coefficient 0.001, group size 5, up to 4 search turns.
- **Workload per step:** 70 prompts in 14 rollout calls of 5 prompts (350 samples), one call per rollout executor, so no executor runs two calls back to back. The trainers train on about 140 to 200 of those samples per step (samples with a usable advantage).
- **Cluster:** two 8-GPU nodes, one executor manager and one object cache on each, the session manager on a CPU node. Flame is installed with the Helm chart from `main` images. The maximum executors per node is 8, matching the GPUs per node, and the object cache's open-file limit is raised to 524288 (`objectCache.openFiles`).
- **Services:** the rollout service has `warmup=14` and requests 1 GPU, 8 CPUs and 64 GiB. The trainer service has `autoscale=False, warmup=2` and requests 1 GPU, 16 CPUs and 128 GiB. Sessions are named after their service (`sr1-train-rollout-…`, `sr1-train-trainer-…`).
- **Gang of two (trainers):** the trainers use **gang scheduling for `min_instances`** (RFE591, #593). With `autoscale=False, warmup=2` the trainer session has `min_instances = max_instances = 2`, and the scheduler's Dispatch binds a session to executors only when it can bind its whole minimum at once, all or none. Both trainers therefore start together, and none of the two `join` calls waits for a partner that never gets an executor. The gang guarantees that both executors are bound, not which nodes they land on: in the 100-step run both were on one node. The rank of each trainer does not come from the gang; it is the position of the executor id in a sorted list kept in a cache object.
- **Driver:** runs inside the cluster, in the `flame-console` pod.
- **Retrieval:** a BM25 index of the questions' paragraphs, built inside each rollout executor.

## Results

### Accuracy

Exact match on the same 200 held-out questions, evaluated greedily:

| Step | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Held-out exact match | 0.145 | 0.330 | 0.400 | 0.410 | 0.450 | 0.475 |

A second evaluation after the last step gave 0.47 (the same weights; the 0.005 difference is run-to-run nondeterminism in generation, which I did not investigate). Mean training reward over each block of 10 steps:

| Steps | 1-10 | 11-20 | 21-30 | 31-40 | 41-50 | 51-60 | 61-70 | 71-80 | 81-90 | 91-100 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Reward | 0.33 | 0.42 | 0.46 | 0.52 | 0.50 | 0.56 | 0.55 | 0.55 | 0.59 | 0.58 |

Behaviour changed as the Search-R1 protocol intends: searches per answer rose from 1.04 (steps 1 to 10) to 2.02 (steps 51 to 100), and the share of samples that produce an answer stayed between 0.94 and 0.98. The KL to the reference model rose from 0.01 to about 0.2 by the end; with a coefficient of 0.001 it exerts almost no pull, so it is a measure of drift, not a constraint.

### Throughput

| Metric | Steps 1-10 | Steps 11-50 | Steps 51-100 | Steps 3-100 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Step time | 28.4 s | 27.3 s | 21.9 s | 24.3 s |
| Rollout phase | 17.2 s | 16.8 s | 12.2 s | 14.3 s |
| Policy update (driver view) | 9.1 s | 10.1 s | 9.3 s | 9.5 s |
| Slowest generate call | 13.9 s | 15.3 s | 10.6 s | 12.8 s |

The whole run (100 steps, 7000 prompts, 35000 samples) took 2471 s of step time, about 41 minutes, or 2.8 prompts per second. Steps get faster later (the slowest generate call shortens from 15.3 s to 10.6 s) although responses get longer (183 tokens in steps 1 to 10, 210 in the last 10); I did not find the cause.

The previous report's delta-sync run took 38.4 s per step with 64 prompts on 6 rollout executors that autoscaled to 8. This run took 24.3 s per step with 70 prompts on 14 fixed rollout executors. The two runs differ in more than the executor count (different cluster build, balanced calls, a fixed pool instead of autoscaling), so the comparison is a rough indication, not a controlled measurement of any one change.

### Data movement

Per step, in steady state (steps 11 to 100):

| Movement | Time | How it is measured |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Trainer publishes the weight delta (encode and upload) | 0.4 s | measured on the trainer |
| Rollout executor downloads the delta | 0.9 s | measured on the slowest executor |
| Rollout executor loads it into vLLM | 1.7 s | measured on the slowest executor |
| Delta published per step | about 165 MB of 6.17 GB | measured |
| Training batch put in the cache and fetched by the trainers | at most about 0.1 s | bounded: the driver's update call equals the trainer's own compute time |
| Trajectories written by rollouts and read by the driver, plus dispatch | at most about 2 s | bounded: rollout phase minus the slowest generate call |

The last two rows are bounds, not measurements. Together data movement is roughly 4 to 5 s of a 24 s step.

### GPU utilization

All 16 GPUs are held for the whole run, but they are not all busy. The step is synchronous, so the two trainer GPUs wait during the rollout phase (about 14 s of 24 s) and the 14 rollout GPUs wait during the update and publish (about 10 s of 24 s). From the measured phases, the GPUs do useful work for at most about half of the GPU-seconds (at most 14 × 12.8 s generating plus 2 × 9.5 s updating, out of 16 × 24.3 s). The balanced call count removed the straggler tail inside the rollout phase (slowest generate 12.8 s against a 14.3 s phase); the remaining idle time comes from the synchronous design. Overlapping the next step's rollouts with the current update would recover most of it, at the cost of one step of policy lag; this was not tried.

What the log shows beyond the tables above:

- **Both trainers ran on one node.** The first lines say `2 trainer executor(s) on 1 node(s)`. Gang scheduling guarantees that both trainers are bound, not which nodes they land on, so the NCCL group in this run used intra-node links only. A 3-step smoke run earlier placed them on two nodes and trained normally, but the 100-step run did not exercise cross-node NCCL.
- **The first two steps pay for the first weight sync.** Step 1 publishes the whole model (6.17 GB, 15.4 s of export) and step 2 is the first time the rollout executors download it (17.5 s) and load it (18.4 s); from step 3 on, only the delta moves (about 0.9 s and 1.7 s). Those two steps took 50.8 s and 43.7 s.
- **The log is not cut off:** the last lines are the step 100 training line and the final evaluation, `eval_em` 0.47.

## Possible improvements (not tried)

These are projections from the phase timings above. None has been run, so the gains are estimates.

The 24.3 s step is serial: the rollout phase (14.3 s) leaves the 2 trainer GPUs idle, and the update (9.5 s) plus weight publish (0.4 s) leave the 14 rollout GPUs idle.

| Change | Expected effect | Cost and risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overlap rollouts and update (one-step-off-policy): generate step k+1 with the weights of step k-1 while step k trains | Step time about the longer phase, about 15 s instead of 24 s (about 1.6 times the throughput); rollout GPUs busy about 85 to 90 % of the time, trainers about 60 % | One step of policy lag, so the loss should clip or correct by an importance ratio. Not checked at this learning rate. About 30 lines in `train.py` as an opt-in flag |
| Colocate generation and training on every GPU: a B200 has memory for a 3B vLLM engine and a trainer replica, so all 16 GPUs generate, then all 16 update | About 14 s per step by the same arithmetic, with no policy lag | A Flame executor hosts one service instance, so both would live in one instance: a larger redesign. Worth it only if the lag above turns out to matter |
| Smaller rollout calls (for example 2 prompts, about 35 calls) so fast executors take more work | Up to about 1.5 s of the 14.3 s rollout phase (the slowest generate call is 12.8 s); the average generate time was not recorded, so the real gain is unknown | Smaller batches per vLLM call |
| Prefetch the next weight delta in the background during generation, and apply it between calls | Hides the 0.9 s download and part of the 1.7 s load, about 2 to 3 s per step (about 10 %); stacks with the overlap | Executors keep one more delta in memory |
| Fetch each rollout result as it finishes, not in call order | Part of the up to 2 s of trajectory transfer and dispatch, which was not measured | Measure first |

The split of 2 trainers and 14 rollout executors is already close to balanced. With overlap the rollout phase (12.8 s) is the bottleneck and the update (9.5 s) is shorter, so a third trainer would only add idle time. This assumes the update scales linearly with the replicas, which was not measured.

The first step is to measure what is missing: the average generate time per call, and the time to write and read trajectories and the training batch. They are a few lines in `rollout.py` and `train.py`, and they would show how much the last two rows are worth.

## Open problems

- **Closed sessions are never deleted.** `flmctl` and the Python SDK close sessions but cannot delete them, although the server has a `DeleteSession` RPC. Closed sessions pile up in `flmctl list -s`; the ones from this work were removed by calling the RPC directly. Tracked in issue #594.
- **A `helm upgrade` stalls the session manager.** The deployment uses a rolling update with a ReadWriteOnce volume, so the new pod cannot attach the volume while the old one runs (Multi-Attach error). Scaling the deployment to 0 and back to 1 works around it; a `Recreate` strategy would avoid it.

## Limitations

- One run, one seed. The accuracy figures come from a 200-question held-out set (standard error about 0.035).
- Exact match is measured with the example's own normalisation over a corpus built from the question paragraphs, so it is not comparable with published Search-R1 results.
- Synchronous on-policy training only. Data-parallel with 2 replicas of 1 GPU each; one model size.
- Both trainer replicas ran on the same node, so the run did not exercise NCCL between nodes.
- The comparison of step times with the previous report is not controlled.
- Trajectory and training-batch transfer times are bounded from other measurements, not timed.
- The weight-delta saving was not re-measured against the full-weights variant in this run.

## Reproduce

The example is `examples/rl/search-r1`. The driver ran in the cluster (the `flame-console` pod) from a virtual environment with the SDK and `datasets`:

```sh
python src/train.py --model Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct --steps 100 \
    --prompts-per-step 70 --prompts-per-task 5 --group-size 5 \
    --rollouts 14 --trainers 2 --out /root/demo3
```

The cluster was installed from `charts/flame` with `cluster.limits.node.maxExecutors=8` and `objectCache.openFiles=524288` in addition to the usual GPU node selectors, tolerations and in-memory cache volume. Per-step metrics are in `metrics.jsonl` in the output directory; `checkpoint.json` names the published weights (100 delta parts under `sr1-models/…`).
